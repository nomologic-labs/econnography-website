## Overview

- Who: Farms across the world
- What: Due to extreme climate change across the world, wheat, barley, and maize crops have decreased between 4–13%. Dry air influenced this, and countries failed to expect the decline. Through the decline of cultivation, it is possible to understand how supply challenges and price increases influence real life. Atmospheric dryness reduces yields even without rainfall decline.

## Definition

- This relates to the key economic concept of scarcity. As a result of global warming, crop yields worldwide—particularly for key grains like wheat, barley, and maize—are significantly reducing. The grains then become scarce.
- Air dryness: Air dryness is different from just not raining, which means drought. The concept of air dryness is the difference between the amount of moisture the air can hold when it is saturated and the amount of moisture currently in the air.
- Supply curve: A graph showing the relationship between the price of a good and the quantity producers are willing to supply.
- Demand curve: A graph showing the relationship between the price of a good and the quantity consumers are willing to buy.
- Non-price determinants of supply: Factors other than price that can shift the entire supply curve left or right. These include input costs, technology, number of producers, government policy, and natural or climate conditions.
- Market equilibrium: The point where the supply curve and demand curve intersect, meaning the quantity supplied equals the quantity demanded, resulting in a stable market price and quantity.

## Cause

- Rising temperatures and increasing dryness are taking a major toll on global crop yields, particularly for key grains such as wheat, barley, and maize. Heat and drought destroy crops and reduce the amount farmers can harvest. This is a worsening of natural factors, which is a non-price determinant of supply.

## Consequences

- Because of the worsening of natural conditions (a non-price determinant of supply), the supply curve shifts leftward. This results in excess demand (a shortage). Suppliers respond by raising the price from P to P1. The increase in prices prices some consumers out of the market, moving the quantity from Q to Q1. This changes the equilibrium point from (Q, P) to (Q1, P1).

## Stakeholders

### Consumers

- Consumers face higher prices for foods like bread, cereal, and other grain-based products. Lower-income consumers suffer the most, spending a larger share of their income on food.

### Government

- Governments may need to increase subsidies or support programs to help struggling farmers. They may also need to import more food from other countries to make up for the domestic supply shortfall, which can strain national budgets.

### Producers

- Producers (farmers) produce a smaller quantity of crops despite spending the same or more on resources such as water, labour, or equipment, meaning their costs per unit go up.

## Long run vs Short run impacts

### Short-run

- Crop supply immediately falls as harvests are damaged by heat and drought.
- Prices rise sharply as existing food stocks are depleted.
- Quantity demanded decreases because of the increase in price.
- There is a new equilibrium point.

### Long-run

- Farmers may start growing more heat-resistant crops.
- Farmers may exit the market entirely.
- Without action, supply will continue to decrease over time.

## Pros & Cons

### Pros

- Higher prices may increase revenue for some farmers.

### Cons

- Undermining global adaptation: Since accurate modelling is essential for designing adaptations, withholding information could cause other countries’ agricultural strategies to miss the mark, leaving them vulnerable to unexpected drying trends.
- Monopolising or early access to drought information by certain countries or businesses could threaten global food security by manipulating grain prices or pursuing huge profits through hoarding.
- Higher food prices may lead to reduced real income.
